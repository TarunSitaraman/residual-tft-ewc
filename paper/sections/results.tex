\emph{Summary.} The proposed model is the only one that does well in both settings (Table~\ref{tab:headline}; pairs such as 0.32/0.31 give the main and replicate seed sets), while each traditional forecaster fails in one of them. With groups arriving in sequence, the proposed model has the lowest error for horizons 1--3, level with linear extrapolation, and is 0.13--0.41~pp more accurate than gradient boosting (replicated; not planned in advance for the shrunk model). On an unseen condition, extrapolation falls behind and the proposed model is 0.43--0.68~pp more accurate (replicated); gradient boosting, which sees every condition's data at once, is best there. Under our model's rule (one condition at a time, no earlier data kept), gradient boosting refitted on the latest condition is slightly more accurate for horizons 1--3 (0.06/0.08~pp, replicated) and indistinguishable for 1--6 (supplement). On the stream, however, gradient boosting refitted on the newest condition forgets (0.17--0.34~pp) and is 0.24--0.46~pp less accurate than the proposed model (replicated, post hoc). Among methods that keep no earlier data, only the proposed model is within 0.07~pp of the best in both settings (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a,b).

\emph{Data.} The S1 diagnostics follow the current SOH closely (median within-cell $\rho=0.97$ for the main incremental-capacity peak) but say little about the change to the next RPT (median $|\rho|\le0.3$). Every S1 sample stays in class C0, while all S6 cells pass from C0 to C3.

\subsection{Continual stream (P1)}
\label{sec:res-p1}

\emph{S1.} All methods perform about the same. TFT\,+\,EWC reaches 0.462~pp (skill 0.27), level with gradient boosting, TFT fine-tuning and MLP replay (0.466--0.475~pp; Table~SIV of the supplement); ridge on the planned usage alone reaches 0.450~pp, and forgetting is small throughout (0.016--0.040~pp).

\emph{S6.} When tasks differ in usage, forgetting is large (0.18--0.46~pp). EWC lowers the TFT's error compared with fine-tuning (0.677 against 0.883~pp), although which differences were significant changed between runs (\S\ref{sec:res-repro}). The MLP beats the TFT in both runs (0.531--0.540~pp), learning in sequence costs the TFT about 0.25~pp compared with joint training, and linear extrapolation (0.343~pp) and gradient boosting (0.372~pp) are best.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Unseen cell or usage condition (P2)}
\label{sec:res-p2}

Gradient boosting is best on both datasets (Table~SVII of the supplement). On S6 the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition is the hardest for every method except extrapolation. With six or seven held-out groups, no difference from the TFT survives Holm correction.

Gradient boosting also has the lowest MAE (0.15 and 0.49~pp on S1 and S6) and the largest $R^2_\Delta$ (0.54, 0.74), while extrapolation predicts the change no better than its average ($-1.31$, $-0.05$). On S6 the plain $R^2$ is at least 0.95 for every method, persistence included, so it does not tell them apart, and the MLP and TFT overestimate SOH (bias $+0.22$, $+0.24$~pp), the optimistic side for reuse. The S6 reuse classes give the same ranking (Table~\ref{tab:d30_cls_s6}; MCC 0.93 for gradient boosting, 0.72 for persistence).

\emph{Reproducibility.}\label{sec:res-repro} A re-run of P1 and P2 in a fixed software environment, on data rebuilt from the raw archives, gave the same baselines, P2 results and ranking of methods, but moved the neural models by up to 0.059~pp. That was enough to change the significance of differences of 0.005--0.014~pp, and led to the stricter tuning and replication rule from P3 on.

\subsection{First-life pretraining (P3)}
\label{sec:res-p3}

Pretraining on the first-life pool (36{,}711 samples, against 2{,}409 in S6) does not help. The TFT and the MLP are both better on average when trained from scratch (TFT 0.401/0.415~pp against 0.509/0.591~pp when pretrained with EWC, significant only in the replicate set), and linear extrapolation (0.343~pp) stays the most accurate method, on S6 and on the pool's own test split (supplement).

\emph{Why the TFT improved.} An ablation added afterwards, and repeated on the replicate seeds, explains why the TFT trained from scratch improves from 0.891 to 0.401~pp with the shared inputs: dropping the 17 diagnostic features (0.17--0.35~pp) and adding the calendar time between checks (0.19--0.31~pp), both replicated ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}\le0.002$).

\subsection{Learned residual on linear extrapolation}
\label{sec:res-residual}

Learning the residual~\eqref{eq:residual} (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c; Table~SIII of the supplement) did \emph{not} meet the criterion registered in advance, beating linear extrapolation. The two are practically equivalent for horizons 1--3; for 1--6 extrapolation is slightly better on average (not replicated) and better at $h=1$--2 (replicated), and the gap closes by $h=5$--6. The residual form does make the TFT competitive: it is 0.17~pp (E1) and 0.24--0.30~pp (E2) more accurate than the direct form and ahead of gradient boosting, all replicated. Over horizons 1--6, where it forgets more, EWC lowers its forgetting from 0.28/0.22 to 0.09~pp (replicated); its effect on the final error does not replicate. For horizons 1--3, EWC and fine-tuning are practically equivalent. \emph{Ageing-cycle inputs.} The 21 inputs extracted with Spark (Section~\ref{sec:spark}), added as planned, make the residual TFT\,+\,EWC worse: 0.480/0.501 against 0.339/0.314~pp, and worse than linear extrapolation, both replicated.

\subsection{Shift-adaptive EWC and literature baselines}
\label{sec:res-d24}

The second registered criterion, for SA-EWC (Table~SIII of the supplement), was \emph{not} met either: although the shift weights varied (0.05--1.14), SA-EWC and a fixed $\lambda$ are practically equivalent in final error and forgetting (differences of at most 0.03~pp). For horizons 1--3, the BiLSTM and Transformer with EWC are practically equivalent to the residual TFT; for 1--6 it beats the residual BiLSTM and TCN (replicated) but not the Transformer. Every model fitted once on all tasks is worse than the residual TFT\,+\,SA-EWC (replicated), except the residual SVR for horizons 1--6.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Shrinkage and intervals on the stream}
\label{sec:res-d25}

Shrinking the correction per horizon makes the residual TFT\,+\,EWC practically equivalent to extrapolation at every horizon (0.318/0.308 against 0.343~pp for horizons 1--3, 0.498/0.493 against 0.485~pp for 1--6) and removes its loss at $h=1$--2, but the registered criterion was again not met. Its 90\% intervals are narrower than extrapolation's but cover too little (0.71--0.81, against 0.92--0.95), so the interval scores are equal. Pretraining the residual model on the first-life pool makes it worse for horizons 1--3 (by 0.22~pp, replicated) and does not help for 1--6.

\subsection{Residual model on unseen conditions}
\label{sec:res-d26}

On unseen conditions (Table~\ref{tab:headline}; Table~SV of the supplement), the shrunk residual TFT\,+\,EWC beats extrapolation, as registered, at origins with a fade rate (by 0.12~pp for horizons 1--3 and 0.18--0.20~pp for 1--6, replicated). At a cell's first two checks there is no fade rate yet, so extrapolation reduces to persistence and loses by over 2~pp. Gradient boosting, however, is 0.39--0.41~pp (horizons 1--3) and 0.57--0.60~pp (1--6) more accurate than the TFT (replicated), on six of the seven conditions; only on the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition is the TFT better. The same residual TFT trained jointly improves on its continual form by 0.32--0.44~pp (replicated), and shrinkage makes no practical difference.

The capacity-based classes give the same ranking (Table~SVI and Fig.~S9 of the supplement). The proposed model puts 95.5\% of held-out samples in their measured class, against 93.9\% for extrapolation and 85.8\% for persistence, while ridge (96.5\%) and gradient boosting (97.7\%) do better. Every error is off by one class. The most common one is also the risky one for reuse: a sample measured as C1 but forecast as C0 (65 of 182 for extrapolation, 42 for the proposed model, 7 for gradient boosting).

\emph{Intervals on unseen conditions.} Intervals calibrated on validation rows of the training conditions are too narrow on the held-out one (coverage 0.66--0.68 for the TFT and 0.74--0.76 for extrapolation, at 90\%). The choice of calibration rows is much of the cause: calibrating extrapolation, which has nothing to fit, on every origin of those conditions raises its coverage to 0.89--0.90. Gradient-boosting intervals calibrated by leaving one condition out reach the registered 0.85 (0.90) with the lowest interval scores (3.2 and 6.7~pp), though their advantage over calibrated extrapolation is significant only for horizons 1--3, so it is not established. The pooled coverage hides one failure: the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition, outside the range of the others, is covered only 0.48 and 0.38 (Fig.~S8).

\emph{Refit and tracking.} Refitting gradient boosting as each check of the held-out condition arrives, and adjusting the interval half-width after every hit or miss, lowers its mean 90\% interval score by 29\%: from 3.22 to 2.29~pp for $h{=}1$--3 (lower on six of seven conditions, $p=0.047$) and from 6.67 to 4.74~pp for $h{=}1$--6 (seven of seven, $p=0.016$), with pooled coverage 0.90--0.91. On the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition coverage rises from 0.48/0.38 to 0.72/0.58, still short of the registered 0.80. Of the held-out intervals, 87--93\% then lie within one reuse class, and 99.2--99.7\% of those name the right class.
