\emph{Summary.} The proposed model is the only one that does well in both settings (Table~\ref{tab:headline}, Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a,b; pairs such as 0.32/0.31 give the main and replicate seed sets). On the stream it has the lowest error for horizons 1--3, level with extrapolation, and is 0.13--0.41~pp more accurate than gradient boosting (replicated; not planned in advance for the shrunk model). On an unseen condition it is 0.43--0.68~pp more accurate than extrapolation (replicated), and gradient boosting with every condition stored is best. Held to our rule (one condition at a time, no earlier data kept), gradient boosting is slightly better on unseen conditions for horizons 1--3 (0.06/0.08~pp, replicated) and level for 1--6, but on the stream it forgets (0.17--0.34~pp) and is 0.24--0.46~pp worse (replicated, post hoc). Only the proposed model is within 0.07~pp of the best in both settings.

\emph{Data.} The S1 diagnostics follow the current SOH closely (median within-cell $\rho=0.97$ for the main incremental-capacity peak) but say little about the change to the next RPT (median $|\rho|\le0.3$). Every S1 sample stays in class C0, while all S6 cells pass from C0 to C3.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Continual stream (P1)}
\label{sec:res-p1}

\emph{S1.} All methods perform about the same: TFT\,+\,EWC reaches 0.462~pp, level with gradient boosting, fine-tuning and MLP replay (0.466--0.475~pp; Table~SIV of the supplement), and forgetting is small (0.016--0.040~pp).

\emph{S6.} When tasks differ in usage, forgetting is large (0.18--0.46~pp). EWC lowers the TFT's error against fine-tuning (0.677 against 0.883~pp; significance changed between runs, \S\ref{sec:res-repro}), the MLP beats the TFT (0.531--0.540~pp), and extrapolation (0.343~pp) and gradient boosting (0.372~pp) are best.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Unseen cell or usage condition (P2)}
\label{sec:res-p2}

Gradient boosting is best on both datasets (Table~SVII of the supplement). On S6 the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition is the hardest for every method except extrapolation. With six or seven held-out groups, no difference from the TFT survives Holm correction.

Gradient boosting also has the lowest MAE and the largest $R^2_\Delta$ (supplement), and the S6 reuse classes give the same ranking (Table~\ref{tab:d30_cls_s6}).

\emph{Reproducibility.}\label{sec:res-repro} A re-run of P1 and P2 in a fixed software environment, on data rebuilt from the raw archives, kept the baselines and ranking but moved the neural models by up to 0.059~pp, changing the significance of differences of 0.005--0.014~pp; hence the stricter tuning and replication rule from P3 on.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{First-life pretraining (P3)}
\label{sec:res-p3}

Pretraining on the first-life pool (36{,}711 samples, against 2{,}409 in S6) does not help: the TFT and the MLP are better trained from scratch, and linear extrapolation stays the most accurate method (supplement).

\emph{Why the TFT improved.} A later ablation, repeated on the replicate seeds, explains the TFT's gain from 0.891 to 0.401~pp: dropping the 17 diagnostic features (0.17--0.35~pp) and adding the calendar time between checks (0.19--0.31~pp), both replicated.

\subsection{Learned residual on linear extrapolation}
\label{sec:res-residual}

Learning the residual~\eqref{eq:residual} (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c) did \emph{not} meet its registered criterion of beating extrapolation: the two are practically equivalent for horizons 1--3, and for 1--6 extrapolation is better at $h=1$--2 (replicated). It does make the TFT competitive: 0.17--0.30~pp more accurate than the direct form and ahead of gradient boosting (replicated). Over horizons 1--6, EWC cuts its forgetting from 0.28/0.22 to 0.09~pp (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}d, replicated; its effect on the final error does not replicate). The 21 Spark inputs make it worse (0.480/0.501 against 0.339/0.314~pp, replicated). \emph{SA-EWC and literature backbones.}\label{sec:res-d24} SA-EWC and a fixed $\lambda$ are practically equivalent, and no literature backbone beats the residual TFT for both horizon sets (supplement).

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Shrinkage and intervals on the stream}
\label{sec:res-d25}

Shrinking the correction makes the model practically equivalent to extrapolation at every horizon (0.318/0.308 against 0.343~pp for horizons 1--3, 0.498/0.493 against 0.485~pp for 1--6), short of the registered criterion; its 90\% intervals cover too little (0.71--0.81). Pretraining on the first-life pool makes it worse (0.22~pp, replicated).

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Residual model on unseen conditions}
\label{sec:res-d26}

On unseen conditions (Table~\ref{tab:headline}; Table~SV of the supplement), the shrunk model beats extrapolation at H$^+$ origins, as registered (0.12--0.20~pp, replicated); at H$^-$ origins extrapolation reduces to persistence and loses by over 2~pp. Gradient boosting (0.39--0.60~pp, on six of the seven conditions) and the joint TFT (0.32--0.44~pp) are more accurate (replicated). The model puts 95.5\% of held-out samples in their measured reuse class (extrapolation 93.9\%, gradient boosting 97.7\%), every error off by one class.



\emph{Intervals.} Calibrated on the training conditions, 90\% intervals cover too little on the held-out one (0.66--0.68 for the TFT, 0.74--0.76 for extrapolation). Calibrated by leaving one condition out, gradient boosting reaches the registered 0.85 (0.90), except on the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition, outside the others' range (0.48, 0.38). Refitting it at each new check lowers its interval score by 29\%.

Under the continual constraint, the proposed model beats gradient boosting refitted on the latest condition (Table~\ref{tab:many}, Fig.~\ref{fig:many}): on S7 for horizons 1--3 only (1.30--1.32~pp; for 1--6 not replicated, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.10$), and on S10, shrinking only at H$^+$ origins, on all 64 conditions for both horizon sets (0.83--0.85~pp, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-10}$), its first registered win over a traditional forecaster. Gradient boosting with every condition stored remains better on most conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:many}b); its higher S10 mean comes from a few calendar conditions where it fails (up to 17~pp), so the S10 verdict for horizons 1--3 is not counted as a win.

On S10, V2 answers all five registered questions positively (Table~\ref{tab:d38}); on S7, Q3--Q5 are positive and Q1 and Q2 negative, as gradient boosting with all conditions stored and GB-ER32 are better (replicated). The S10 gains for Q1 and Q2 come mostly from the conditions where gradient boosting fails (all stored, horizons 1--6: mean 4.77, median 0.83~pp): the median differences are small (Q1: $-0.02$ to $-0.03$~pp for horizons 1--3, $-0.13$ to $-0.14$~pp for 1--6; Q2: $-0.05$ to $-0.06$ and $-0.18$ to $-0.19$~pp), and gradient boosting with all conditions stored is better on 22--23 of 64 conditions for 1--3. On S7 it is better on 43--51 of 60 conditions, most at H$^-$ origins, and the two boosting comparators are better by 0.27--0.87~pp on average. V2 shows no replicated difference from the joint TFT, which stores all data. Replay gives most of the gain over the EWC model (0.26--0.81~pp, replicated); the ensemble adds 0.05--0.14~pp, and calibration leaves the error unchanged. Coverage is uneven by ageing type: 0.82/0.83 on S10 calendar conditions for horizons 1--6, 0.81--0.84 on S7 cyclic ones.
