\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\input{tables/headline}

\emph{Summary.} Neither traditional forecaster is reliable in both settings (Table~\ref{tab:headline}). Gradient boosting, which sees every condition's data at once, is the most accurate on an unseen condition but 0.13--0.41~pp less accurate than the proposed model on the continual stream (replicated). Linear extrapolation matches the proposed model on the stream but is 0.43--0.68~pp less accurate on an unseen condition (replicated). The proposed residual TFT-EWC is level with or better than each where the other fails. Under the same constraint as the proposed model (conditions one at a time, earlier data not kept), gradient boosting refitted on the latest condition is level with it for horizons 1--6 and slightly better for 1--3 (0.06/0.08~pp); warm-started boosting cannot use the usage inputs after the first condition and is far worse (details in the supplement).

\emph{Data.} S1 diagnostics track the current SOH (median within-cell $\rho=0.97$ for the main incremental-capacity peak) but not the change to the next RPT (median $|\rho|\le0.3$); every S1 sample stays in class C0, while all S6 cells cross from C0 to C3.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Continual stream (P1)}
\label{sec:res-p1}

\emph{S1.} TFT\,+\,EWC reaches 0.462~pp (skill 0.27), level with gradient boosting, TFT fine-tuning and MLP replay (0.466--0.475~pp; Table~SIV of the supplement); a ridge regression on the planned usage alone is slightly better (0.450~pp). Forgetting is small for every method (0.016--0.040~pp). Differences whose significance changed in a re-run are only 0.005--0.014~pp (\S\ref{sec:res-repro}): on S1 the methods are practically equivalent.

\emph{S6.} When tasks differ in usage, forgetting is substantial (0.18--0.46~pp). EWC lowers the TFT's error and forgetting relative to fine-tuning (0.677 against 0.883~pp), but which difference is significant changed between runs (\S\ref{sec:res-repro}). MLP fine-tuning and MLP\,+\,EWC (0.531 and 0.540~pp) are significantly better in both runs, the joint upper bounds (0.414 and 0.427~pp) put the TFT's continual-learning cost at about 0.25~pp, and linear extrapolation (0.343~pp) and gradient boosting (0.372~pp) are the best forecasters.

\subsection{Unseen cell or usage condition (P2)}
\label{sec:res-p2}

\input{tables/p2}

Gradient boosting is best on both datasets (Table~\ref{tab:p2}). On S6 the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition is the hardest for every method (1.8--2.4~pp). With six or seven held-out groups, no difference with the TFT survives Holm correction.

The further metrics (D30, from a re-run within 0.0052~pp of Table~\ref{tab:p2}; full sets in the supplement) give the same ranking: gradient boosting has the largest $R^2_\Delta$ (0.54 on S1, 0.74 on S6), while linear extrapolation forecasts the change no better than its mean ($-1.31$ and $-0.05$; persistence $-1.85$ and $-3.16$).

\emph{Reproducibility.}\label{sec:res-repro} A re-run of P1 and P2 in a pinned environment on data rebuilt from the raw archives reproduced every baseline, every P2 result and the ranking of methods; the neural models moved by at most 0.059~pp, but the significance of differences of 0.005--0.014~pp changed (details in the supplement). This motivated the ten tuning orders, the one-standard-error rule and the replication rule used from P3 on: neural training is deterministic per platform, not across platforms.

\subsection{First-life pretraining (P3)}
\label{sec:res-p3}

Pretraining on the first-life pool (36{,}711 samples, against 2{,}409 in S6) does not help: for the pretrained TFT, EWC and fine-tuning are practically equivalent, both backbones are better from scratch on average (TFT 0.401/0.415~pp against 0.509/0.591~pp pretrained with EWC, significant only in the replicate set), and linear extrapolation (0.343~pp) remains the most accurate method, on S6 and on the pool's own test split (details in the supplement).

\emph{Why the TFT improved.} A post hoc ablation, repeated on the replicate seeds, explains why the TFT from scratch improves from 0.891 to 0.401~pp with the shared inputs: dropping the 17 diagnostic features (0.17--0.35~pp) and adding the calendar time between checks (0.19--0.31~pp), both replicated ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}\le0.002$).

\subsection{Learned residual on linear extrapolation}
\label{sec:res-residual}

  \label{fig:d23bar}

For the residual form~\eqref{eq:residual} (Fig.~\ref{fig:d23bar}; Table~SIII of the supplement), the pre-registered criterion, residual TFT\,+\,EWC beating linear extrapolation, was \emph{not} met: the two are practically equivalent for horizons 1--3 (differences of $-0.004$ and $-0.029$~pp), and for horizons 1--6 extrapolation is better on average ($+0.076$ and $+0.049$~pp, not replicated). Per horizon, extrapolation is better at $h=1$ and 2 (replicated), and the gap closes without reversing by $h=5$--6. Learning the residual does make the TFT competitive: it improves TFT\,+\,EWC over the direct form by 0.17~pp (E1) and 0.24--0.30~pp (E2) and puts it ahead of gradient boosting, all replicated. For horizons 1--6, where it forgets more, EWC lowers its forgetting from 0.28/0.22 to 0.09~pp and its backward transfer from 0.18/0.14 to 0.03~pp, replicated; its effect on the final error is not. For horizons 1--3, EWC and fine-tuning are practically equivalent. \emph{Ageing-cycle inputs.} The 21 Spark-extracted inputs (Section~\ref{sec:spark}), added as pre-specified, make the residual TFT\,+\,EWC worse: 0.480/0.501 against 0.339/0.314~pp, and worse than linear extrapolation, both replicated.

\subsection{Shift-adaptive EWC and literature baselines}
\label{sec:res-d24}

The second pre-registered criterion, for SA-EWC (Table~SIII of the supplement), was \emph{not} met: the residual TFT with SA-EWC and with fixed $\lambda$ are practically equivalent in final error and forgetting for both horizon sets (differences of at most 0.03~pp), although the shift weights varied (0.05--1.14). For horizons 1--3, BiLSTM\,+\,EWC (direct, 0.323~pp, and residual) and the residual Transformer\,+\,EWC are practically equivalent to the residual TFT; for 1--6 it beats the residual BiLSTM and TCN (replicated; not the Transformer). Every single-fit regressor is worse than the residual TFT\,+\,SA-EWC (replicated), except the residual SVR for horizons 1--6.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Shrinkage and intervals on the stream}
\label{sec:res-d25}

Shrinking the correction per horizon makes the residual TFT\,+\,EWC practically equivalent to extrapolation at every horizon (0.318/0.308 against 0.343~pp for horizons 1--3, 0.498/0.493 against 0.485~pp for 1--6), removing its replicated loss at $h=1$--2; the correction is turned down most at $h=1$ (Fig.~S7 of the supplement). The criterion was again not met. Its 90\% intervals are narrower than extrapolation's but under-cover (0.71--0.81, against 0.92--0.95), so the interval scores are equal (practically equivalent or not replicated). Pretraining the residual model on the first-life pool makes it worse for horizons 1--3 (by 0.22~pp, replicated) and does not help for 1--6.

\subsection{Residual model on unseen conditions}
\label{sec:res-d26}

In the fourth experiment (Table~\ref{tab:headline}; Table~SV of the supplement), as registered, the shrunk residual TFT\,+\,EWC beats extrapolation at origins with a fade rate, by 0.12/0.12~pp for horizons 1--3 and 0.20/0.18~pp for 1--6 (replicated): the first comparison in which the proposed model beats extrapolation. Elsewhere extrapolation is persistence and loses by over 2~pp. Gradient boosting, however, beats the TFT by 0.39/0.41~pp and 0.57/0.60~pp (replicated), on six of the seven conditions; only on the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition is the TFT better. The same residual TFT trained jointly on the six conditions beats its continual form by 0.32--0.44~pp (replicated). Shrinkage and the raw correction are practically equivalent here.

The reuse classes follow the same ranking (Table~\ref{tab:d26_classes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:d26cm}). The proposed model assigns the measured class to 95.5\% of held-out samples (94.4--96.8\% over the 50 runs), against 93.9\% for extrapolation and 85.8\% for persistence, but ridge (96.5\%) and gradient boosting (97.7\%) do better. All errors are between neighbouring classes; most forecast a measured C1 sample one class healthier (C0): 65 of 182 for extrapolation, 42 for the proposed model and 7 for gradient boosting.

\emph{Intervals on unseen conditions.} Intervals calibrated on validation rows of the training conditions under-cover on the held-out one (pooled 0.66--0.68 for the TFT, 0.74--0.76 for extrapolation); the calibration rows, not only the model, cause the shortfall. With leave-one-condition-out calibration, gradient-boosting intervals reach the registered 0.85 (0.90) with the lowest interval scores (3.2 and 6.7~pp), but not on the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition, outside the range of the others (0.48 and 0.38; details and Fig.~S8 in the supplement).

\emph{Refit and tracking.} Refit and tracking lower the mean 90\% interval score by 29\%, from 3.22 to 2.29~pp ($h{=}1$--3; six of seven conditions, $p=0.047$) and from 6.67 to 4.74~pp ($h{=}1$--6; seven of seven, $p=0.016$), with pooled coverage 0.90--0.91. On the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition coverage rises from 0.48/0.38 to 0.72/0.58, short of the registered 0.80. With these intervals, 87--93\% of held-out forecasts lie within one reuse class, 99.2--99.7\% of them correctly.
