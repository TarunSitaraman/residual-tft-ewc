\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{Why the proposed model does not win.} On the continual stream TFT\,+\,EWC never beat linear extrapolation. Fade over one to three checks is smooth, so extrapolating the recent fade rate leaves little structure for a sequence model to add; the groups are few and small (21--27 and 135--225 training samples per task in S1 and S6); and the TFT barely uses its history window. EWC behaves as expected where there is something to protect: forgetting is negligible across the near-identical S1 cells and substantial across the S6 usage conditions. Even there its benefit to the direct TFT did not survive a re-run, and after pretraining validation selected almost no EWC. Its clearest benefit came once the TFT learned only the correction to extrapolation: over six horizons, EWC cut that model's forgetting by about two thirds, replicated. Making the penalty follow the measured shift between groups added nothing, plausibly because with seven tasks and a tuned $\lambda_0$, per-pair weights between 0.05 and 1.14 change the protection little compared with the choice of $\lambda$ itself (an interpretation, not tested). The backbone matters less than the target: at horizons 1--3 a BiLSTM or Transformer with EWC is as accurate as the residual TFT, which pulls ahead only over six horizons. On unseen conditions, where extrapolation is weaker, the residual TFT beats it, but the continual constraint itself becomes the main cost: trained jointly, the same model is 0.32--0.44~pp more accurate and close to gradient boosting. A model that is never shown the new condition gains nothing from adapting and still pays for the order of the stream. With battery datasets this small, storing every earlier group is cheap, so the constraint that motivates continual learning did not bind here.

\emph{What matters.} Building on the best simple forecaster mattered most: the learned residual made TFT\,+\,EWC one of the most accurate learned models on the S6 stream and level with extrapolation for horizons 1--3. The ablation also points to the inputs rather than the architecture: the diagnostic features hurt the TFT, and the calendar time between checks helped, consistent with fade per cycle rising with that time at 50~$^\circ$C in all 20 S6 cells. Usage known in advance is a strong predictor, and forecasts are conditional on it. For intervals, the rows used for calibration mattered as much as the model: residuals from mid-life validation origins of conditions seen in training understate the error on a new condition, while residuals from all origins of conditions held out from fitting reproduce it. Dense measurements between checks did not help: with few cells per condition, 21 extra inputs added more variance than information. Pretraining on 369 first-life cells of other chemistries did not transfer to the LFP cells of S6, although retention of the pool was not the problem.

\emph{Reproducibility.} Several significance results changed in a re-run on different hardware (Section~\ref{sec:res-repro}); small datasets, many low-variance runs and Holm-corrected tests make such fragility likely. We recommend recording the full software environment, tuning with enough runs to separate neighbouring grid values, and requiring that claimed differences replicate on a second seed set and exceed a practically meaningful threshold.

\emph{Reuse and positioning.} The reuse-class mapping is informative only when the forecast horizon crosses class boundaries: S1 stays in C0 and the S6 stream's test periods lie in C3, while on unseen S6 conditions the classes agree for 95--97\% of samples with the learned models. Intervals make the mapping usable for decisions: a forecast whose interval lies within one class (87--92\% of samples) was almost always right, and the others are flagged as uncertain rather than assigned. For a battery population unlike any seen before, such as the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition here, neither the forecasts nor the intervals can be trusted, and pooled coverage does not reveal it. The studies of Section~II estimate the current SOH or SOC on other cells and with other SOH definitions, so their errors are not directly comparable with ours.

\emph{Limitations.} The task was changed to forecasting after the first dataset was inspected; the second-life datasets are small; S6 is aged at 50~$^\circ$C and its ``second life'' continues the same protocol; SOH is relative to each cell's first valid measurement; forecasts are conditional on planned usage; and the ablation and every later experiment were decided after the main results (all except the ablation were specified and committed before they were run). The single-fit regressors see all tasks at once, and the direct literature backbones were run for horizons 1--3 only. The TFT hyperparameters used on unseen conditions were tuned with validation rows of all seven S6 conditions, and the unseen-condition results rest on seven conditions from one laboratory, with the conditions fixed and only training randomness varied. Leave-one-condition-out intervals were run for gradient boosting and extrapolation only.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We built a leakage-controlled benchmark for forecasting the SOH of second-life lithium-ion cells from five public datasets and evaluated a Temporal Fusion Transformer with Elastic Weight Consolidation as a continual learner, with every follow-up experiment pre-registered and every claimed difference replicated on a second seed set. Learned forecasters beat persistence clearly, and forgetting appears whenever usage conditions differ. Learning a shrunk correction to linear extrapolation made TFT\,+\,EWC as accurate as extrapolation on the continual stream, where no model beat it, and more accurate than extrapolation on unseen usage conditions. There, however, gradient boosting fitted on all conditions and the same TFT trained jointly were clearly better: at this data scale, the cost of learning continually outweighed its benefit. Shift-adaptive EWC, first-life pretraining, literature backbones and dense ageing-cycle inputs did not help. Conformal intervals held on unseen conditions only when calibrated on conditions held out from fitting, and not for a condition outside the training range; with them, 87--92\% of forecasts fell within one reuse class, over 99\% of those correctly, and the rest were flagged as uncertain. Future work should test continual learning where earlier data cannot be stored and populations are larger and more varied, and should calibrate intervals across laboratories and chemistries.
