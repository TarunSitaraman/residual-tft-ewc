\section{Introduction}

When an electric-vehicle (EV) battery can no longer meet the demands of driving, it can often still serve as stationary storage or backup power. Reusing it extends its life and delays recycling, but only if its health can be judged reliably.

The state of health (SOH) compares a battery's present capability with a reference state. Machine-learning models have estimated it from voltage, current, temperature, partial-charging and impedance measurements~\cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4}. Retired batteries, however, differ in chemistry, maker, capacity, past use, temperature and charging, and their records from first use are often incomplete, so a model trained once may fail on the next group. Continual learning updates a model with each new group while trying to keep what it learned before. It must be tested on old and new groups alike to show whether it forgets.

This work brings together continual learning, health forecasting and a first screening step for reuse. Rather than estimate today's SOH, we \emph{forecast} it one to six capacity checks ahead from everything measured up to the latest check. On our first dataset, estimating the current SOH from cycling data did worse than simply repeating the last measured value (\S\ref{sec:methods}), and a reuse decision depends on how a battery will age, not only on its health today. The forecast is then sorted into capacity-based classes that support, but do not replace, a full reuse assessment.

Our contributions are as follows (Fig.~\ref{fig:system}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item A benchmark for forecasting the SOH of second-life cells from five public datasets (395 cells after quality checks), built so that no future information reaches the inputs. Two label flaws are removed: test currents set from the previous capacity, and pulse charge counted as capacity.
  \item A comparison of a Temporal Fusion Transformer with Elastic Weight Consolidation (TFT\,+\,EWC) with fine-tuning, replay, training on all data at once, simple forecasters and the model types of earlier work (BiLSTM, TCN, Transformer, GPR, random forest, SVR) in three settings: groups arriving in sequence, groups never seen in training, and pretraining on first-life data. We report how well each method adapts and how much it forgets.
  \item Residual TFT-EWC, which corrects linear extrapolation instead of replacing it. Among methods that keep no earlier data, it is the only one close to the best in both settings: it matches extrapolation on the stream, where EWC cuts its forgetting by about two thirds, outperforms it on unseen usage conditions, and beats gradient boosting refitted on each new condition on the stream (replicated).
  \item Forecast intervals for unseen usage conditions: calibrated on the training conditions they are too narrow, while calibrating on conditions left out of training restores the stated coverage, except outside the training range.
  \item A reproducibility check: several significance results on these small datasets changed with the computing platform, so our main results must pass a replication rule.
\end{itemize}
