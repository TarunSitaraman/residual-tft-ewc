This supplement gives the preprocessing, the input correlations, the training curves and the full classification and
regression metrics behind the paper. Every figure and table is generated by a script in the repository from committed
results or from the public data; the script is named in each caption. SOH is relative to each cell's first valid
capacity check. The additional results (last section) back the paper's main finding: of the models compared, Residual
TFT-EWC is the only one that stays competitive both on the continual stream (Table~\ref{tab:residual}) and on unseen
usage conditions (Table~\ref{tab:d26}), while each traditional forecaster falls behind in one of the two settings.

\section{Preprocessing}

Preprocessing is the same for every model and uses no label information beyond the SOH history at the origin. It
begins by verifying the raw files against their repository record or checksums; our parsing reproduces the processed S1
RPT capacities and the authors' capacities of all 843 S6 checks exactly. Validity rules then remove unusable checks: an
S6 check is used only if its C/25 discharge starts from full charge and reaches the cut-off, which 4 of 847 checks fail
(Fig.~\ref{fig:prep}c), and in S1 RPT~2 (run at about 20~$^\circ$C) is dropped.

Noise is reduced only where differentiation would amplify it. Incremental-capacity curves $-\mathrm{d}Q/\mathrm{d}V$ are
computed on a 1~mV (S6) or 2~mV (S1) voltage grid after a Gaussian filter of the charge (5~mV in S6, 10~mV in S1);
without this filter the measurement noise dominates the curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:prep}b). Peak positions and heights are
taken from the smoothed curve, and in S6 a peak at the edge of its window is set to missing. This is the only
filtering; no other denoising or outlier removal is applied.

The ageing cycles and the model inputs are prepared last. Apache Spark reduces $9.0\times10^{8}$ per-second rows of the
S6 ageing cycles to block inputs (mean, last value and slope of seven responses per 100-cycle block). Features never
observed are dropped (e.g.\ the second IC window in S6), missing values are imputed with the training median and
flagged, and inputs are standardised. Imputation, scaling and target scaling are fitted on the first task and then
frozen.

\section{Input correlations}

Fig.~\ref{fig:corr} gives the Spearman correlation of the S6 history and diagnostic inputs (F1) with each other and with
the target. Ten of the 17 diagnostics are near-monotone functions of the SOH at the origin ($|\rho|\ge0.9$), so they add little

\section{Training curves}

Each task is trained with Adam (batches of 64) for at most 200 epochs, with early stopping on the task's validation
RMSE (patience 20); the weights of the best epoch are kept. Fig.~\ref{fig:epochs} shows one stream of the residual
TFT\,+\,EWC (horizons 1--3, first evaluation order, seed 0, tuned learning rate $3\cdot10^{-3}$ and $\lambda=1$). The
re-run reproduces the committed final average error of that stream exactly (0.464~pp). Validation RMSE reaches its
minimum within 2--30 epochs on five tasks and after 86 epochs on task 6; on task 5 no epoch improved on the
weights carried over from task 4, which were therefore kept.

\section{Ageing-cycle processing and first-life pretraining}

\emph{Spark job.} Between checks, every S6 cell logged each ageing cycle at about one sample per second: 827 files,
132~GB raw and $9.0\times10^{8}$ rows. Apache Spark~4.2 (PySpark, local mode) reduces them to block-level inputs: one map
task per 100-cycle block derives, for every cycle, a validity flag and seven measured responses (end-of-charge and
end-of-discharge voltage, constant-voltage hold time and final current, relaxation after charge and after discharge,
internal resistance); Spark SQL groups the cached per-cycle table (82{,}529 cycles) by cell and block into the mean,
last value and slope of each response (21 inputs), and each block joins the origin at the check that ends it. Charge,
energy and current are excluded because the protocol scales them with the previous capacity (within-cell
$r=0.97$--$0.999$ with the previous check's capacity). The run takes 20~min on six cores and matches an independent
pandas implementation on all 827 blocks (largest difference $2\times10^{-13}$).

\emph{First-life pretraining (P3).} The pool provides 36{,}711 samples, against 2{,}409 in S6, and validation selected
EWC $\lambda=0.01$ for the pretrained TFT. Under the replication rule, EWC and fine-tuning are practically equivalent for
the pretrained TFT (differences of 0.015--0.017~pp). The S6 stream does not degrade the pool test error
(TFT\,+\,EWC: 3.280 to 3.148~pp), but on that split the pretrained models are also less accurate than extrapolation
(3.001~pp): pretraining does not learn a first-life model worth transferring.

\emph{Attention.} The TFT's attention over the four previous origins is nearly uniform (0.24--0.26 per lag): it makes
little use of its history.

\section{Additional results}

Tables~\ref{tab:residual} and \ref{tab:d26_classes} and Figs.~\ref{fig:d25}, \ref{fig:d27}, \ref{fig:d26cm} and \ref{fig:d23bar} belong to Sections IV-D to IV-G of the paper and
were moved here for space.

\emph{Reproducibility of the comparisons (Section IV-B).} P1 and P2 were re-run in a pinned environment on data rebuilt from the raw archives. Every baseline, every P2 result and the ranking of methods reproduced; the neural models moved by at most 0.059~pp. On S1, five Holm-corrected comparisons with TFT\,+\,EWC crossed $p=0.05$. On S6, validation selected EWC $\lambda=1000$ instead of 100 (a 0.008~pp tie), and the error advantage of TFT\,+\,EWC over fine-tuning moved from $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.005$ to 0.060, while its reduction of forgetting moved from 0.050 to 0.008. These findings motivated the ten tuning orders, the one-standard-error rule and the replication rule used from P3 on. A D30 re-run of P2 on another platform reproduced every baseline exactly and moved the MLP and TFT by at most 0.027~pp per held-out group: neural training is deterministic per platform, not across platforms.

\emph{Reuse classes in the D30 re-run of P2.} On S6 the learned models assign the measured reuse class to 95--97\% of samples (MCC 0.90--0.93), against 94\% (0.88) for linear extrapolation and 86\% (0.72) for persistence, and all errors but one (persistence) are between neighbouring classes; the end-of-first-life decision does not separate the methods (AUC $\ge$0.998), as 69\% of S6 samples are below 80\%.

\emph{Reuse classes on unseen S6 conditions (Section IV-F of the paper).} The proposed model puts 95.5\% of held-out samples in their measured class, against 93.9\% for extrapolation and 85.8\% for persistence, while ridge (96.5\%) and gradient boosting (97.7\%) do better (Table~\ref{tab:d26_classes}, Fig.~\ref{fig:d26cm}). Every error is off by one class. The most common one is also the risky one for reuse: a sample measured as C1 but forecast as C0 (65 of 182 for extrapolation, 42 for the proposed model, 7 for gradient boosting). The pretrained TFT reaches 0.509/0.591~pp against 0.401/0.415~pp from scratch (significant only in the replicate set).

\emph{Intervals on unseen conditions.} Intervals (Fig.~\ref{fig:d27}) calibrated on the pooled validation rows of the training conditions under-cover on the held-out one, for the TFT (pooled 0.66--0.68) and for extrapolation (0.74--0.76) alike. Calibrating extrapolation, which has no fitted parameters, on all origins of those conditions instead of their mid-life validation rows restores 0.89--0.90: the calibration rows caused the shortfall, not only the model. With leave-one-condition-out calibration, gradient-boosting intervals reach the registered threshold of 0.85 (0.90 in both horizon sets) with the lowest mean interval scores (3.2 and 6.7~pp, against 7.1 and 11.8~pp for calibrated extrapolation and 6.1--6.3 and 10.0--10.3~pp for the TFT), lower than calibrated extrapolation's on six of seven conditions (significant only for horizons 1--3, $p=0.047$, against $p=0.30$, so not established). Coverage holds over conditions, not for each: on the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition, outside the range of the others, it is 0.48 and 0.38.

\emph{Continual stream (Table~\ref{tab:residual}).} Learning the residual makes the TFT the most consistent continual learner: for horizons 1--3 it is level with linear extrapolation and with the best literature backbones, and for horizons 1--6 it is ahead of the residual BiLSTM and TCN (replicated). With EWC its forgetting over six horizons is about a third of fine-tuning's (0.09 against 0.22--0.28~pp) and lower than that of every other backbone (0.13--0.35~pp). Gradient boosting, SVR and GPR, although fitted on all tasks at once, are less accurate on average.

\emph{Unseen conditions (Table~\ref{tab:d26}).} The proposed model is more accurate than linear extrapolation and persistence on all origins and on origins with a fade rate. Gradient boosting and the jointly trained TFT are more accurate still, but both are fitted on all six training conditions at once; under the continual constraint, gradient boosting is within 0.08~pp (see below). The table lists the shrunk residual TFT\,+\,EWC; the raw correction gives 0.88/0.90, 0.79/0.80, 1.59/1.62 and 1.46/1.49~pp in the same columns.

\emph{Shift-adaptive EWC and literature backbones (Sections III-E and IV-D of the paper).} The models fitted once see all tasks at the same time, and the direct literature models were run for horizons 1--3 only. The second registered experiment of the paper lets the EWC strength follow how far the data shift between groups (shift-adaptive EWC, SA-EWC): when task $t$ starts, the EWC penalty (Eq.~(4) of the paper) of each finished task $j$ is multiplied by $d_{j,t}=\big((\lVert\mu_t-\mu_j\rVert^2+\lVert\sigma_t-\sigma_j\rVert^2)/D\big)^{1/2}$, the per-dimension 2-Wasserstein distance between diagonal Gaussians fitted to the two tasks' preprocessed training inputs, with $\lambda_0$ tuned like $\lambda$. BiLSTM, TCN and Transformer-encoder continual learners (TFT input window, within 20\% of its parameters) and GPR, random forest and SVR fitted once are run in residual and direct form. The second registered criterion, for SA-EWC (Table~\ref{tab:residual}), was \emph{not} met either: although the shift weights varied (0.05--1.14), SA-EWC and a fixed $\lambda$ are practically equivalent in final error and forgetting (differences of at most 0.03~pp). For horizons 1--3, the BiLSTM and Transformer with EWC are practically equivalent to the residual TFT; for 1--6 it beats the residual BiLSTM and TCN (replicated) but not the Transformer. Every model fitted once on all tasks is worse than the residual TFT\,+\,SA-EWC (replicated), except the residual SVR for horizons 1--6.

\emph{Many unseen conditions (S7 and S10, Section IV-G of the paper).} Both tests were registered before any model was fitted on the dataset (D34, D36) and were motivated by the wish to find a setting where the proposed model beats the traditional forecasters. S10 (Stroebl et al., 2024; Samsung INR21700-50E) has 279 cells in two stages; condition = (stage, test point), since test-point names repeat across stages. Its label is the discharge capacity of the last complete 1C\,+\,CV discharge of each 23~$^\circ$C check-up; check-ups were dropped for surface temperature outside 18--28~$^\circ$C at its start (20), dates out of order (13), no complete discharge (3) or no usable date (3), and cells with fewer than five valid check-ups (66 calendar cells have only the entry and final tests) are not used. On S7 the registered model shrinks at every origin; at H$^-$ origins this pulls the forecast towards persistence and made it worse than the raw model (E1: 6.66 against 4.43~pp on H$^-$), so the S10 test shrinks only at H$^+$ origins. On S10 this rule is practically equivalent to shrinking everywhere, and better than the raw model for horizons 1--3 only ($-0.07$~pp). Against gradient boosting refitted on the latest condition, the S10 model is better at both H$^+$ ($-0.94$, $-1.07$~pp) and H$^-$ origins ($-0.61$, $-0.31$~pp; E1, E2; replicated). On S7 the advantage is at H$^+$ ($-2.2$ to $-2.3$~pp) while the model is worse at H$^-$ ($+1.4$ to $+2.1$~pp). Gradient boosting with every condition stored has a larger error than the proposed model on S10 calendar conditions (E2 mean 12.5 against 1.4~pp) and a smaller one on cyclic conditions (1.25 against 1.61~pp). In the registered rule the direction comes from the mean difference, so for S10, horizons 1--3, the test against it reads ``better'' although the median difference ($+0.17$~pp) and 52 of 64 conditions favour gradient boosting; we do not count it as a win. The tuning chose learning rates of $10^{-5}$ to $3\cdot10^{-3}$ and EWC strengths of $10^{-6}$ to $1$, mostly far below the S6 values, so in most folds EWC adds little penalty. Pooled 90\% coverage of the intervals is 0.71/0.57 (S7) and 0.74/0.43 (S10) for E1/E2, below the 0.85 the registration required.

\emph{Refit and tracking (Section IV-F of the paper).} In the sixth experiment on unseen conditions the held-out condition arrives over time: as each target is measured, gradient boosting is refitted with it and the half-width moves by $c\,q_h(e-a)$, with $e=1$ for a miss (quantile tracking; $c$ chosen on the training conditions). Refitting gradient boosting as each check of the held-out condition arrives, and adjusting the interval half-width after every hit or miss, lowers its mean 90\% interval score by 29\%: from 3.22 to 2.29~pp for $h{=}1$--3 (lower on six of seven conditions, $p=0.047$) and from 6.67 to 4.74~pp for $h{=}1$--6 (seven of seven, $p=0.016$), with pooled coverage 0.90--0.91. On the 100\% depth-of-discharge condition coverage rises from 0.48/0.38 to 0.72/0.58, still short of the registered 0.80. Of the held-out intervals, 87--93\% then lie within one reuse class, and 99.2--99.7\% of those name the right class.

\emph{Gradient boosting under the continual constraint (D32).} Most of gradient boosting's advantage on unseen conditions comes from storing every training condition. The headline comparison of the paper (its Table~III) therefore also trains gradient boosting like the proposed model: the six training conditions arrive one at a time and earlier conditions' data are not kept. Under this constraint the two are close. Refitted on the latest condition only, gradient boosting reaches 0.86 and 1.59~pp (horizons 1--3 and 1--6), against 0.92/0.94 and 1.62/1.65~pp for the proposed model (slightly better for 1--3, $+0.06/+0.08$~pp, replicated; not distinguishable for 1--6), whereas with all six conditions stored it reaches 0.54 and 1.05~pp. Table~\ref{tab:p1} is the first P1 benchmark (Section IV-A of the paper). Warm-started condition by condition (200 more trees per condition), it reaches 2.65 and 3.49~pp, and the proposed model is better by 1.7--1.9~pp (replicated). This gap is mostly an artefact: scikit-learn keeps the bin edges of the first condition when warm-starting, and every usage input is constant within a condition, so the later trees cannot use them. We therefore do not count it as evidence against continual gradient boosting in general.
